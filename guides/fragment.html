Admissions, explained simply

Top 6 average

Most Ontario universities calculate your admission average from your six best Grade 12 U or M courses, and the program’s required courses (prerequisites) must be among them. For example, a program might require English (ENG4U), Advanced Functions (MHF4U) and Calculus (MCV4U), then add your three best remaining 4U/4M courses.

Early in the year, universities often use your Grade 11 final marks and Grade 12 midterm or first-semester marks instead, which is why early offers can arrive before you have final grades. Some programs adjust averages (for example, Waterloo applies an adjustment factor to some programs). Each program page states exactly what it uses.

OUAC 101 vs 105 (Group A vs Group B)

The Ontario Universities’ Application Centre (OUAC) has two main undergraduate applications. OUAC 101 (Group A) is for students currently attending an Ontario high school. OUAC 105 (Group B) is for everyone else, such as students from other provinces, international students and people who already graduated.

On this site, “Group A” and “Group B” labels come straight from applicants’ submissions. See ouac.on.ca ↗ for the official definitions.

When offers come out

Universities send offers on a rolling basis. Early offers can start in the fall for strong applicants, while many programs make most decisions between February and May. Applications submitted by each university’s “equal consideration” date (often in January) are considered equally.

Use the Offer tracker to see what applicants reported this cycle compared with last year. For official dates, check OUAC ↗ and each university’s admissions site.

Conditional offers

Most offers are conditional: you must finish your courses and usually keep a minimum final average (sometimes in specific courses too). Your offer letter and the university’s applicant portal list the exact conditions. Falling below them can lead to an offer being rescinded, so read the letter carefully.

Deferred, waitlisted and alternate offers

Deferred means the university hasn’t decided yet and will review you again later, often with newer marks. Waitlisted means you may get an offer if space opens. An alternate offer is an offer to a different program than the one you applied to. On this site these stay separate from offers and rejections.

AIF (Admission Information Form)

The AIF is the University of Waterloo’s form where applicants share information beyond grades, such as activities, achievements and interests. Some Waterloo programs require it and others strongly recommend it. See Waterloo future students ↗ for current details.

Kira

Kira is an online assessment with timed video and/or written responses that some programs use to learn about applicants’ communication and personal qualities. Programs that use it tell you in their supplementary application instructions.

Casper

Casper is an online situational judgment test, where you respond to scenarios, used by some programs (often in health or education) as part of admissions. Check each program’s requirements for whether it is needed.

Supplementary applications

Many competitive programs ask for a supplementary application: extra questions, essays, videos, interviews or portfolios. These often have their own deadlines, earlier than you might expect, and can matter a lot alongside your average. The “What applicants mentioned” section on each program page shows what people reported.

IB and AP students

Universities convert IB and AP results in their own ways (for example using predicted IB scores for early offers). Averages reported here as “42/45 IB” are kept as written and aren’t converted into percentages, so they don’t appear on percentage charts. Check each university’s IB/AP admission page.

Reading this site

Everything on Offer Atlas is self-reported and unverified. A median reported offer average is not a cutoff, and people who post aren’t a random sample (people with good news post more often). Use it as context alongside official requirements, not as a prediction.
